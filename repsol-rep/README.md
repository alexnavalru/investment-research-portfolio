# Repsol (REP) — Peak-Cycle FCF Bear Case

**Case type:** Bearish / Cyclical Normalization / Commodity & Refining-Margin Mean Reversion  
**Status:** Short watchlist; preferred entry around **€30/share**  
**Target price:** **below €24/share**  
**Reference price:** approximately **€29–€30/share** in early October 2026  
**Primary skills:** commodity-cycle analysis, refining economics, normalized FCF, accounting adjustments, scenario valuation, catalyst/risk analysis

## Executive Summary

Repsol is a much better company than it was in prior cycles: management is more disciplined, the balance sheet is stronger and shareholder remuneration through dividends and buybacks has improved materially.

That is precisely why the bearish thesis is **not** a thesis that Repsol is a poor business.

The thesis is that the market is valuing a cyclical company close to record share-price levels while 2026 earnings and cash generation are being boosted by an unusually favorable energy environment.

At roughly **€29–€30/share**, my current high-energy-price scenario produces about **€6.3bn of FCF**, or approximately **€5.7/share**, making the stock appear to trade near **5x current-year FCF**.

I do not view that multiple as genuinely cheap because the denominator is unusually elevated.

My normalized valuation is **below €24/share**.

### Current Return Bridge

Using the **October 6, 2026 close of €28.76/share** and Repsol's **€1.051/share total cash dividend paid in 2026** (~**3.7%** of the October 6 share price), an illustrative move to **€24/share** would imply:

**Price return: -16.6% | Dividend contribution: +3.7% | Illustrative total return: ~-12.9%**

Because my normalized thesis value is **below €24**, €24 is the upper end of the bear-case valuation rather than the final downside target. If the share price fell below €24, the negative price contribution would be larger.

## Why the Current Earnings Level Looks Cyclically Elevated

Repsol benefits from several parts of the energy value chain:

- upstream oil and gas production;
- refining;
- chemicals;
- customer / mobility;
- low-carbon generation.

In 2026, two of the most important earnings drivers — crude prices and refining economics — have moved well above the assumptions used by the company in its strategic planning.

### Repsol's 2026 planning case vs. my energy scenario

Repsol's early-2026 guidance used **$60–65/bbl Brent**, **$3.5–4.0/MMBtu Henry Hub**, and a **$6.5–7.5/bbl Refining Margin Indicator**. Management also mentioned a budgeted refining premium of approximately **$1.4/bbl** (around **$1.4–1.5/bbl** in my working assumptions), giving a **combined economic refining-margin range of about $7.9–9.0/bbl**. The March 2026 strategic update's central case specifically assumed **$65 Brent, $4.0 Henry Hub and a $6.5/bbl Refining Margin Indicator**. The indicator and the additional premium are different components.

My model uses the following **full-year 2026 averages**:

| 2026 variable | Repsol planning / central reference | My model scenario |
|---|---:|---:|
| Brent ($/bbl) | $60–65 (central $65) | **~$91.2** |
| Henry Hub ($/MMBtu) | $3.5–4.0 (central $4.0) | **~$4.0** |
| Refining Margin Indicator ($/bbl) | $6.5–7.5 (central $6.5) | Not isolated in my total-margin spreadsheet |
| Budgeted refining premium ($/bbl) | ~$1.4–1.5 | Included in total margin |
| **Total economic refining margin ($/bbl)** | **~$7.9–9.0** (model base **$8.6**) | **~$29.5** |

This comparison matters because my forecast is not simply assuming a higher oil price: **the largest source of cash-flow upside comes from refining margins far above normal planning levels**.

#### Estimated CFFO uplift from the macro scenario

My spreadsheet applies its own base values and linear sensitivities to the full-year averages:

| Driver | Model base | My 2026 estimate | Model annual CFFO uplift |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Brent | $65.0/bbl | ~$91.2/bbl | **~€656m** |
| Henry Hub | $4.0/MMBtu | ~$4.0/MMBtu | **~€0m** |
| Total economic refining margin | $8.6/bbl | ~$29.5/bbl | **~€4,184m** |
| **Total modeled uplift** | | | **~€4,839m** |

For the calculation I used **€250m of annual CFFO per $10/bbl Brent**, **€130m per $0.5/MMBtu Henry Hub**, and **€200m per $1/bbl refining margin**. The line items are rounded; the total comes from the model's unrounded quarterly calculations. Repsol's *published* 2026–2028 sensitivity for Brent is **€285m per $10/bbl**, not the €250m used in my sheet. More importantly, Repsol's refining sensitivity is for the **Refining Margin Indicator at its Spanish industrial complexes**, not necessarily for each extra dollar of the **all-in economic margin** including premium.

The **~€4.84bn** is therefore a **directional gross sensitivity estimate, not company guidance** and not a directly additive forecast of reported CFFO. Repsol originally guided to **€5.5–6.0bn CFFO** under its lower energy assumptions; my separate adjusted cash-flow model estimates roughly **€10.2bn of CFFO** in the high-price case after its own accounting and perimeter adjustments. The difference between those values should not be interpreted as a precise reconciliation.

## Refining Is the Core of the Bear Case

The refining business is currently producing economics that are difficult to treat as a normal mid-cycle run rate.

Repsol's 2026 results have shown unusually strong margins, driven by:

- tight middle-distillate markets;
- crude-slate optimization;
- discounted heavy crude inputs;
- higher jet-fuel economics;
- biofuel economics;
- trading and logistics optimization;
- geopolitical supply disruption.

Repsol also distinguishes between its reported **Refining Margin Indicator** and an additional **premium** captured through optimization and other effects.

This matters because simply annualizing a crisis-period refining margin can dramatically overstate normalized earnings power.

Official sensitivity is also material: Repsol estimates that each **$1/bbl change in refining margin** changes annual CFFO by roughly **€200m** for its Spanish industrial complexes.

My scenario assumes a **~$29.5/bbl all-in economic margin**, compared with a **~$8.6/bbl all-in planning baseline**. That gap highlights the scale of the potential normalization, although the company's **€200m/bbl sensitivity applies to its refining-margin indicator**, not automatically to the premium component.

## Oil Prices: Strong Current Cash Flow, Weak Normalization Signal

The upstream business is also benefiting from a price environment far above the company's central planning case.

On October 7, 2026, Brent was trading around **$100–101/bbl**, while the company's strategic central assumption for 2026 was **$65/bbl**.

The current geopolitical environment includes continuing supply risk related to the Middle East and Russia/Ukraine. Those risks can remain elevated for longer than expected, so the bear case does **not** depend on predicting an immediate collapse in oil prices.

The argument is simpler:

**a cyclical valuation should be based on mid-cycle economics rather than extrapolating wartime / disruption-driven commodity prices indefinitely.**

Repsol's own sensitivity framework estimates approximately **€285m of annual CFFO for each $10/bbl change in Brent** over 2026–2028.

## Cash Flow: Why I Do Not Use Reported CFO Mechanically

Repsol's cash-flow analysis requires more care than simply reading consolidated CFO and subtracting capex.

The company has a meaningful amount of activity conducted through joint ventures and **equity affiliates**. Under the new reporting model introduced at the end of 2025, joint-venture contributions are accounted for using the **equity method** rather than proportionate consolidation in segment reporting.

Under IFRS consolidation, the underlying operating cash flows of equity-accounted affiliates are not consolidated line by line into Repsol's cash-flow statement. Cash only reaches the consolidated statement when it is actually distributed or transacted with the parent.

For that reason, my owner-cash framework reconciles:

- reported CFFO;
- interest and lease cash items;
- transactions with non-controlling interests where economically relevant;
- net capex / divestments;
- the economics of non-consolidated affiliates.

This does not mean every cash flow from an affiliate belongs directly to Repsol shareholders. The purpose is to avoid comparing years with different ownership structures and reporting perimeters without adjustment.

## 2026 High-Commodity-Price Scenario

My current internal scenario — based on the much stronger 2026 commodity and refining environment — estimates approximately:

| Metric | 2026 high-price scenario |
|---|---:|
| Adjusted CFFO | **~€10.2bn** |
| Net capex | **~€3.9bn** |
| FCF | **~€6.3bn** |
| FCF / share | **~€5.7** |
| Share price reference | **~€29–€30** |
| P / FCF | **~5x** |

The apparent 5x FCF valuation is the central trap in the bearish thesis.

The stock looks optically cheap **because current FCF is inflated by unusually favorable macro conditions**.

## Normalized Scenario

Using Repsol's own strategic macro assumptions as a normalization anchor:

- Brent around **$60–65/bbl**;
- Henry Hub around **$3.5–4/MMBtu**;
- refining margin around **$6.5–7.5/bbl**, plus a more normal premium;

my internal normalized cash-flow framework is much closer to:

- **~€6.0bn CFFO**;
- **~€3.9bn net capex**;
- **~€2.1bn FCF**;
- approximately **€1.9/share of normalized FCF**.

Applying roughly **10x–12x normalized FCF/share** produces a valuation range of approximately:

**€19–€23/share**

This is the main reason my normalized fair-value estimate is **below €24/share**.

The exact number is less important than the framework: a low multiple on peak earnings is not the same thing as a low valuation on normalized earnings.

## Why I Am Not Structurally Negative on Repsol

Several factors make this a more difficult short than a simple cyclical mean-reversion trade:

- management quality has improved;
- the balance sheet is relatively strong;
- buybacks reduce the share count;
- the dividend is attractive;
- refining assets may deserve structurally better economics than in earlier cycles;
- Repsol's integrated model can capture dislocations that hurt less-flexible competitors;
- oil and refining margins can remain elevated for longer than expected.

Repsol plans to return **30%–40% of CFFO** to shareholders over 2026–2028 through dividends and buybacks.

That makes timing important.

## Bear-Case Catalyst

The main catalyst is **normalization**, not a company-specific collapse.

Potential triggers include:

- de-escalation of the Iran / Middle East conflict;
- improved Russian / global energy supply;
- rebuilding of product inventories;
- normalization of diesel and jet-fuel cracks;
- lower refining premiums;
- Brent reverting toward Repsol's planning range;
- investors shifting from peak reported FCF to normalized FCF.

## Key Risks to the Bear Thesis

- Geopolitical conflicts keeping oil and refined-product prices elevated for years.
- Structural refinery closures making European refining margins permanently higher.
- Repsol continuing to capture a large premium above its refining indicator.
- Strong buybacks materially increasing FCF and earnings per share.
- Better-than-expected upstream production growth.
- New projects improving normalized earnings enough to offset commodity normalization.
- The market continuing to value the company on shareholder yield rather than normalized FCF.

## What This Case Demonstrates

This case demonstrates the difference between **reported cheapness and normalized cheapness**.

The core analytical framework is:

**peak commodity prices + peak refining margins → unusually high FCF → optically low multiple → normalize the cycle → materially lower sustainable FCF → lower fair value**
