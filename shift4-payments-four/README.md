# Shift4 Payments (FOUR) — Investment Post-Mortem

**Case type:** Investment Post-Mortem / Payments  
**Entry:** approximately **$66/share in December 2025**  
**Primary skills:** FCF reconstruction, valuation discipline, capital-structure analysis, error review

## Why I Include a Losing Investment

A research portfolio should not contain only successful investments.

The purpose of this case is to show how I review an investment when the outcome is poor, identify which assumptions were wrong and convert those mistakes into explicit process improvements.

## Original Thesis

The attraction was clear:

- strong revenue growth;
- rapid EBITDA expansion;
- improving scale;
- an attractive payments platform;
- the possibility of continued earnings growth.

I accepted a relatively demanding valuation because I expected the growth profile to continue.

## What I Got Wrong

The central analytical mistake was giving too much weight to growth and adjusted profitability while not being sufficiently strict about **cash ultimately available to common shareholders**.

The revised framework starts from:

**Cash from operations  
– total capital expenditure  
– preferred dividends  
= FCF available to common shareholders**

This matters because a business can report strong EBITDA growth while the value accruing to common equity grows much more slowly.

## Why the Entry Valuation Mattered

At an entry price near **$66**, a high-growth thesis left less room for error.

Once I rebuilt the cash-flow framework, the valuation looked materially more demanding on a common-shareholder FCF basis than the headline growth rates suggested.

The lesson was not simply “do not pay a high multiple.” It was:

> **The stronger the growth narrative, the more important it is to reconcile adjusted metrics with cash available to common equity before deciding what multiple is justified.**

## Process Changes

This investment led me to add several explicit checks to my research process:

1. Reconcile management-defined FCF with CFO and the cash-flow statement.
2. Include all recurring capital expenditure required to operate and grow the business.
3. Treat preferred distributions and other senior claims separately from common equity.
4. Compare EBITDA growth with actual cash conversion.
5. Stress-test the valuation before using growth to justify a premium multiple.
6. Define thesis-breaking conditions before entering the position.

## What This Case Demonstrates

This case is intended to demonstrate analytical accountability rather than investment performance.

A good investment process should be able to explain:

- why a position was initiated;
- which assumptions mattered;
- what was missed;
- how the valuation changed;
- what rule will be different next time.
