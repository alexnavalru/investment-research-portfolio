# UnitedHealth Group (UNH) — Crisis-Driven Fundamental Thesis

**Case type:** Contrarian Fundamental / Temporary Dislocation / Turnaround  
**Research period:** May–July 2025  
**Primary skills:** insurance economics, normalized earnings, management assessment, regulatory analysis, scenario valuation, long-dated options

## Executive Summary

I began researching UnitedHealth Group during the sharp 2025 selloff, when the shares fell from almost **$600 in April** to roughly the **$250–$300 range** as several negative events hit at once.

The market was trying to price:

- a major increase in medical-cost utilization;
- a sharp earnings-guidance reset;
- leadership change at the top of the company;
- regulatory and Department of Justice scrutiny;
- reputational pressure around claims-management practices;
- lingering consequences of the Change Healthcare cyberattack.

My thesis was that the market was treating a severe **pricing and execution problem** as if it were a permanent impairment of the franchise.

The key question was not whether 2025 would be a bad year. It clearly would be. The question was whether UnitedHealth's normalized earning power could recover once pricing, benefits, product design and cost controls were reset.

My base-case valuation was **above $400/share**, with a medium-term range of roughly **$400–$450 by 2027**.

## Why the Selloff Became Interesting

At the start of 2025, UnitedHealth expected adjusted EPS of **$29.50–$30.00**.

On April 17, the company cut that guidance to **$26.00–$26.50**, citing unexpectedly high Medicare Advantage care activity and other reimbursement pressures.

On May 13, it suspended the outlook entirely because medical expenditures were running materially above assumptions.

The problem later became clearer: UnitedHealthcare acknowledged that its pricing assumptions had been **well below actual medical costs**. In its Q2 2025 review, the company estimated roughly **$6.5bn more medical costs** than assumed in the original outlook.

This was a serious underwriting and execution failure. But it also created the central investment question:

> **Were current medical-cost pressures permanently destroying the economics of the business, or had the company temporarily underpriced a cost structure that could be reflected in future premiums, benefits and plan design?**

## Core Thesis: Pricing Should Catch Up to Costs

Health insurers do not have unlimited ability to reprice contracts immediately. There can be a lag between the medical costs experienced today and the rates embedded in existing plans.

That lag was central to my thesis.

UnitedHealth had underestimated 2025 Medicare Advantage medical-cost trend when it designed those products in the first half of 2024. The company later said it had originally assumed just over **5%** medical-cost trend and came to expect approximately **7.5%** for 2025.

My expectation was that this mismatch would eventually be addressed through:

- higher pricing;
- benefit redesign;
- narrower networks and product changes;
- selective market or plan exits;
- more conservative assumptions for medical utilization.

That mechanism was later explicitly confirmed by management. For 2026, UnitedHealth said its pricing strategy was focused on margin recovery, with Medicare Advantage pricing assuming medical trend approaching **10%**. Commercial contracts could be repriced progressively as renewals occurred.

This does **not** mean every cost increase can simply be passed through without consequences. Repricing can reduce membership and regulators can constrain economics. The thesis was that a permanent collapse in industry profitability was less likely than a multi-year reset of pricing and product design.

## Scale as a Competitive Advantage

UnitedHealth was not a marginal insurer entering a price war.

At year-end 2024, UnitedHealthcare served approximately **50.7 million medical members**, while UnitedHealth Group served about **146 million unique individuals across its businesses**.

UnitedHealth was also the largest U.S. commercial health insurer nationally and the largest Medicare Advantage insurer by market share.

That scale matters because it supports:

- national provider networks;
- purchasing and contracting leverage;
- large actuarial datasets;
- diversification across employer, Medicare, Medicaid and health-services businesses;
- the Optum platform across care delivery, pharmacy, data and services.

I would not state that "if UnitedHealth cannot make money, no insurer can." Different insurers have different membership mixes and execution. The stronger version of the argument is:

> **If the largest and most diversified player were structurally unable to earn adequate returns because medical inflation could never be reflected in pricing, that would indicate an industry-wide economic problem. A broad repricing response was therefore more plausible than permanently accepting uneconomic contracts.**

## Stephen Hemsley Was a Major Part of the Thesis

On May 13, 2025, Andrew Witty **stepped down for personal reasons** and Stephen Hemsley returned as CEO.

Hemsley's history was one of the strongest qualitative elements of my thesis.

He had previously served as UnitedHealth CEO from **2006 to 2017**. Reuters reported that UnitedHealth shares rose **nearly four-fold** during that tenure. He was also closely associated with the development of Optum, which became a major growth engine for the group.

His return gave me more confidence that the company could execute a difficult operational reset rather than merely wait for medical trends to improve.

The alignment signal was also unusually strong:

- Hemsley bought **86,700 shares at $288.57**, investing about **$25.0m** of his own money in May 2025.
- President and CFO John Rex bought **17,175 shares at an average $291.12**, investing about **$5.0m**.
- UnitedHealth stated that a significant portion of Hemsley's net worth was already invested in company shares.

For me, this combination of prior operating history, direct knowledge of the company and substantial open-market buying materially improved the turnaround case.

## Regulatory and Reputational Uncertainty

This was an area where different issues needed to be separated carefully.

### Claims denials and public scrutiny

Before the 2025 selloff, the U.S. Senate had already investigated delays and denials of post-acute care in Medicare Advantage, including practices at UnitedHealthcare.

The December 2024 killing of **Brian Thompson, CEO of UnitedHealthcare — the insurance division, not UnitedHealth Group itself —** then intensified public and political scrutiny of health insurers and claims-management practices.

This created an additional uncertainty premium around the stock.

### Department of Justice investigation

The federal investigation reported in May 2025 was **not primarily an investigation into ordinary claim denials**.

The reported DOJ criminal investigation concerned possible **Medicare Advantage fraud and risk-adjustment / billing practices**. UnitedHealth initially said it had not been notified of the reported investigation. In July 2025, it confirmed that it had begun complying with formal criminal and civil DOJ requests relating to aspects of its Medicare participation.

I viewed this as a real risk, but one that had to be valued separately from the medical-cost problem.

## Valuation

My original framework used multiple valuation methods rather than relying on a single earnings number:

- normalized P/E;
- price / free cash flow;
- EV / EBITDA.

The company's 2024 adjusted EPS was **$27.66**. At the start of 2025 it guided to **$29.50–$30.00**, before cutting and then withdrawing that outlook.

I did not assume an immediate return to the original guidance. Instead, the thesis assumed depressed near-term earnings followed by a gradual normalization as pricing and operating changes took effect.

My medium-term target was:

**$400–$450/share by 2027**

The working model cross-checked a 2027 value in roughly the **low-$400s** under its normalized earnings and cash-flow scenarios.

## Position Construction

Because the thesis depended on a multi-year normalization rather than a specific quarterly catalyst, I preferred **long-dated call options** rather than short-duration contracts.

One structure analyzed in the original work was a **December 2027 $290 call** when the shares were around the high-$200s.

The logic was:

- enough time for pricing actions to work through renewals;
- asymmetric exposure to a recovery toward the valuation range;
- defined maximum loss equal to the premium.

The important risk was timing: being correct on normalized value but wrong on how long the repair would take could still make the option lose money.

## Outcome

**Personal trade outcome, unaudited:** the option position approximately **doubled within a few months**.

The stock itself continued to be highly volatile and later traded below the initial research level before the recovery broadened. It ultimately moved back above **$400 in 2026**.

I view the result as evidence that the market was pricing an unusually large uncertainty discount, not as proof that every part of the original thesis was correct.

## Key Risks

- Medical-cost trend proving structurally higher rather than temporarily underpriced.
- Repricing leading to significant membership losses.
- Medicare Advantage reimbursement pressure.
- Medicaid rate mismatches persisting longer than expected.
- Optum Health execution remaining weak.
- DOJ or other regulatory actions causing material financial or structural damage.
- Additional reputational issues around managed-care practices.
- Long-dated options losing value if the recovery took longer than expected.

## What This Case Demonstrates

This case demonstrates how I approached a large-cap company experiencing a rapid narrative break:

**earnings shock → identify temporary vs. structural drivers → study management → test industry economics → normalize earnings → value the recovery → choose an instrument with enough duration**
